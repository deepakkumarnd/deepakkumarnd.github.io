# {post_title}
I learned how to swim around 2010, during my third year of engineering. At the time, I felt I needed to become more physically active. I was spending most of my time being lazy, watching movies, straining my eyes in front of a computer monitor, and playing NFS Most Wanted. I wanted to break that routine and do something different.

I started looking for activities that were inexpensive, didn't depend much on other people, and could be done independently. Running and swimming seemed like good options. Both were challenging, required very little investment, and didn't need anyone else's help.

My college had a decent gym and facilities for other activities like cricket and football, but none of those really appealed to me. I was physically quite weak back then, and honestly, I was a little intimidated by the idea of going to the gym. My eating habits were terrible, too. I knew very little about nutrition or healthy eating, and neither did my parents back then.

I had resilience, but I lacked stamina. More importantly, I lacked the skills and abilities that usually come from playing sports consistently from a young age. Sports were never really encouraged at home, so I missed out on that kind of exposure during my childhood.

The two games I enjoyed playing the most were chess and badminton. I was fairly good at both, but I never had the opportunity or encouragement to take either of them to the next level.

My father taught me chess when I was very young. I was naturally drawn to the game, partly because I had seen it being played in movies. At school, some of the senior students used to play chess during our extended Friday lunch breaks. I would often go and watch them play, trying to understand their moves and strategies.

I also developed my knowledge by reading chess books that my father brought home. Eventually, I started teaching chess to almost everyone around me, including my neighbors and cousins.

Looking back, I think I had a fairly advanced understanding of chess for my age. Many of the adults I played against weren't even familiar with concepts like recording moves, castling, or the English names of the chess pieces. That was probably one of the few areas where I had developed a skill well beyond my years.

Unfortunately, I couldn't continue with either of those two games. During high school, most of my peers were completely focused on academics, and I gradually drifted away from sports and physical activities. From high school until my third year of engineering, a period of almost eight years, I barely engaged in any form of physical activity.

I developed a bit of a belly, which looked rather odd on my otherwise lean frame. I wasn't obese by any means, but I wasn't particularly happy with how my body looked either.

Then, one fine day, I decided to go swimming in a nearby temple pond with a few local friends. It was during a semester break, and I had plenty of free time on my hands.

I grew up surrounded by small rivers and ponds, yet I had never learned to swim. I had always wanted to, but there was one big obstacle: I was terrified of drowning. My mother was particularly protective and would never let me go anywhere near the river. My father had tried teaching me once, but he didn't quite have the patience to continue with the lessons. And so, despite growing up around water, I remained a non-swimmer for years.

But there was an incident that made my fear of water even worse.

Just before my high school days, driven by an overwhelming enthusiasm to learn swimming, I went to a nearby river with a few friends to give it a try. Initially, I stayed close to the shallow end, where I could comfortably stand. But as I kept trying, I unknowingly drifted into a deep canal that ran alongside a paddy field. Before I realized what was happening, I was sinking.

I remember lifting my hand, hoping to reach the water's surface, but it was nowhere close. I wanted to open my mouth and scream for help, to call out to my friends, but I knew I couldn't. I could only see the light filtering down from above. I couldn't find the ground beneath my feet, and I couldn't even position myself upright in the water.

Above me, I could see a boatman rowing past, completely unaware that someone was drowning right beneath him.

For the first time, I felt utterly helpless. Fear slowly began to take over. I genuinely thought this could be the end. Yet, strangely, I didn't panic. I knew I still had some air left in my lungs, perhaps just a few more seconds.

And then, in that terrifying moment, a hand reached down towards me. It grabbed me and pulled me, and then another pair of hands lifted me out of the water. Someone had spotted me and managed to rescue me.
He pushed me towards the shore, which was only a couple of feet away. When I finally reached it, I could stand with the water barely reaching my shoulders.

I was alive. Someone had pulled me out just in time.

I took a huge breath of relief. With my eyes still red, I looked at my rescuer. He was smiling at me and, in a hushed voice so that the others wouldn't notice, shouted, "ചത്താനെല്ലോടാ പന്നി!" — a typical expression in our local slang, roughly translating to, "You almost died, you idiot!"

It was my childhood friend **Ambadi**. He was part of our group and, as it turned out, the unofficial swimming instructor for the day. Out of the three or four kids who had come along, he was the only one who actually knew how to swim.

Somehow, he had noticed that I was missing and figured out that I must have drifted into the deep canal near the paddy field. He went looking for me and managed to pull me out just in time.

Interestingly, I was fully conscious throughout the incident. I hadn't swallowed any water or experienced any breathing difficulty. Before this incident, I had developed a basic understanding that having enough air in your lungs could help you float to the surface. So whenever I attempted to dive underwater, I made sure to take a deep breath first. Even while I was sinking, I knew I still had air in my lungs and believed I could eventually make it back to the surface.

But there was one thing I hadn't accounted for: my legs were stuck deep in the mud at the bottom of the canal. No matter how much I tried, I couldn't push myself upwards.

Thanks to Doordarshan and its yoga programs, I was quite good at practicing pranayama. Holding my breath for a couple of minutes wasn't particularly difficult for me.

Interestingly, this entire episode remained a secret. Nobody outside our small group ever came to know about it. There were two reasons for that. First, my mother would probably have killed me if she had found out that I had gone anywhere near the river in the first place! Second, the whole incident lasted only a couple of minutes, and nobody apart from Ambadi and I really understood how dangerous the situation had been. To everyone else, it was probably just another ordinary day by the river.

That incident left a lasting impression on me. It created a deep fear of swimming in rivers, and I never attempted to swim in one again after that.

Somehow, that fear gradually faded by the time I joined college. I remember visiting a friend's home near Kattakkada, Thiruvananthapuram. During that visit, we all went for a swim in a canal carrying fresh water from the Neyyar Dam for irrigation.

This time, things were different. I spent quite a lot of time in the water, but I was much more cautious and aware of my surroundings. Fortunately, that particular stretch of the canal was relatively safe. It was a good experience, and perhaps the first step towards rebuilding my confidence in the water.

Two years later, during my third year of engineering, I finally decided to give swimming another serious attempt. I started visiting the Srikrishna Temple pond near my college. It was a large, deep pond, and quite a few students from our college used to go there to learn swimming.

Some came with friends, and they helped them learn and practice. I initially started going with a few local friends. When one of my hostel mates heard about it, he decided to join me. His name was Adarsh, and like me, he had only a basic understanding of swimming.

For the next couple of weeks, we went to the pond almost every evening, and sometimes even in the mornings. Initially, we never ventured beyond the stone steps along the boundary of the pond. Just a few feet away from the edge, the water was already incredibly deep.

There were some excellent swimmers who would casually swim diagonally across the pond and back, repeating the journey several times. Watching them was fascinating. For me, what they were doing seemed like a distant dream. I couldn't imagine myself swimming that confidently someday.

There was also a well somewhere near the center of the pond. The experienced swimmers would sometimes take a break by resting on top of it after swimming across. Interestingly, the well wasn't visible from the shore. You could only discover it once you actually swam out to that spot.

Over time, I became quite comfortable in the water, and my swimming skills improved rapidly. I started by swimming 1–2 laps along one side of the pond, then it became 10–12 laps and gradually moved on to swimming two or three round trips diagonally across it. I even got comfortable enough to take a break on top of the well in the middle of the pond.

I felt liberated. In just four to six weeks, I had become a fairly fast swimmer and had picked up several swimming styles, including breaststroke, butterfly, and backstroke. Backstroke quickly became my favorite. It was also my fallback whenever I felt tired or wanted to take a break.

One of my favorite things to do was simply float on my back with my eyes closed, almost as if I were lying on a mat on a sunny afternoon. Except that my mat was the water itself, and I was floating right in the middle of a huge pond! Whenever I got into that pose, I couldn't help imagining Mohanlal floating in the water in the song "Dhyanam Dheyam Narasimham" from Narasimham. 

Spending a couple of hours in the water became effortless. What had once been a source of fear had turned into something deeply satisfying. I had gone from being afraid of water to feeling completely at home in it.

Of course, swimming wasn't without its downsides. I developed an ear infection from repeated exposure to pond water. I also ended up with plenty of scratches and bruises from accidentally bumping into stones near the pond's boundaries. These were small but frequent reminders that spending so much time in a natural pond came with its own share of challenges.

Eventually, I switched to swimming exclusively in clean, well-maintained swimming pools. Looking back, I wouldn't recommend learning to swim on your own, especially in rivers or ponds. Learning without proper guidance can be extremely dangerous, and natural water bodies can also pose hygiene risks.

The ear infection, unfortunately, stayed with me for several years. It would disappear and return periodically, until it finally went away completely.

Despite these challenges, swimming has been an incredibly rewarding experience. It's a wonderful full-body exercise that engages almost every muscle group and demands coordination of your senses. The ability to control your breathing, maintain stability in water, and move freely through the vast waters of our planet is something truly exciting. Perhaps that's an exaggerated way of describing swimming, but that's how it feels to me!

Even today, I occasionally receive compliments from people who watch me swim. I consider that a privilege, and it genuinely makes me happy. What started as an attempt to overcome my physical inactivity, and perhaps even a childhood fear, eventually became a skill that I deeply enjoy and take pride in.
